LEVEL, PROGRESSION & CONTENT DESIGN

How Plants vs. Zombies 2 introduces new problems, composes encounters, escalates difficulty and expands content without forcing the player to learn a complicated control scheme.

01 / DESIGN QUESTION
TEACH → TEST → COMBINE
The core controls are simple. The design challenge is deciding what the player knows, what they can do, and what new problem should come next.

How does the game continuously introduce new problems without continuously introducing new rules?

PvZ2's complexity comes largely from recombining a readable ruleset: plants, zombies, grid space, resources, waves, objectives and world rules.

MOMENT-TO-MOMENTSCAN → PLANT → DEFEND → REACT → ADAPT
LEVEL LOOPENTER → LOADOUT → WAVES → OBJECTIVE → REWARD / FAIL
PROGRESSION LOOPCOMPLETE → EARN → UNLOCK / GROW → FACE NEW CHALLENGE
02 / LEVEL STRUCTURE
THE BOARD IS THE TEACHER
A readable grid lets the designer spend complexity on what appears, where it appears and when it appears.
OBSERVATION

Spatial rules stay stable while encounter rules change.

The lane/grid structure is quickly readable. A player can spend attention on threat evaluation instead of learning movement.

Design takeaway: readable spatial structure gives level design more room to manipulate encounter complexity.

03 / ENCOUNTER COMPOSITION
DIFFICULTY THROUGH COMBINATION
Difficulty can rise through enemy behavior, quantity, lane pressure, timing and combinations rather than raw stat inflation alone.
THREAT 01

BEHAVIOR

A new enemy teaches a readable counter.

THREAT 02

CONTEXT

The same enemy becomes harder when another threat competes for attention.

THREAT 03

PRESSURE

Timing and resource constraints compress the player's decision window.

Threat → Response → Reprioritize

The important level-design move is not merely adding an enemy. It is changing the relationship between threats so the player has to revise their plan.

04 / ENEMY INTRODUCTION
INTRODUCE → PRACTICE → COMBINE
New enemy behavior becomes useful content only after the player has enough context to understand it.

01 · INTRODUCE

Show the behavior with low pressure.

02 · PRACTICE

Allow the player to solve it with known tools.

03 · REINFORCE

Repeat enough to build recognition.

04 · COMBINE

Place it beside familiar threats.

05 · PRESSURE

Shorten reaction time and increase overlap.

05 / PLAYER ABILITY
NEW PLANTS EXPAND THE SOLUTION SPACE

EA describes Seed Packets as a way to strengthen plants, including attacks, defenses, planting speed and new abilities.

The progression value is not only power growth. New capabilities create more ways for the level designer to construct problems.

NEW PLANT→NEW TACTICAL OPTION→NEW ENCOUNTER POSSIBILITY→NEW LEVEL DESIGN SPACE
06 / RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
SUN CREATES A SECOND LAYER OF DIFFICULTY
The player is solving the threat while simultaneously deciding how much resource capacity to commit now versus later.
PLAYER TRADE-OFF

Stabilize now or preserve capacity?

Planting is not only a tactical choice; it is an investment against future pressure.

DESIGN TAKEAWAY

Resource pressure converts pacing into decisions.

A simple resource system can make each wave feel like a sequence of investment and recovery decisions.

07 / OBJECTIVES
OBJECTIVES REFRAME FAMILIAR SYSTEMS
Changing the player's goal changes how the same plants, resources and enemies are prioritized.

SURVIVE

Maintain the defense through the wave.

CONSTRAIN

Work under a plant, resource or board limitation.

COMPLETE

Meet a special condition while continuing to manage the threat.

Design takeaway: objectives are efficient content multipliers because they reshape decision priorities without requiring a completely different ruleset.

08 / DIFFICULTY CURVE
DIFFICULTY ≠ MORE ENEMIES
The curve below is a designer's explanatory model, not measured telemetry. It shows how perceived difficulty can rise by layering enemy complexity, encounter density, resource pressure, timing and combinations.
ABSTRACT PLAYER-DEMAND CURVE · DESIGN MODEL
The important design relationship: the player keeps the same basic plant-placement vocabulary while the level changes the amount of information, timing pressure and simultaneous threat they must process.
COMPLEXITY→DENSITY→RESOURCE PRESSURE→TIMING PRESSURE→COMBINATION

Understand → Practice → Combine → Pressure → Master

A strong difficulty curve increases the number and interaction of decisions, not simply enemy health.

09 / CONTENT PACING
TEACH. TEST. PEAK. RELEASE.
Perceived difficulty depends on the spacing between learning moments, challenge peaks and recovery moments.

TEACH

Introduce one meaningful idea.

TEST

Let the player use it.

MIX

Combine known ideas.

PEAK

Maximize pressure.

RELEASE

Let mastery feel earned.

10 / WORLD PROGRESSION
A NEW WORLD SHOULD FEEL LIKE A NEW DESIGN SPACE

EA's official feature page describes 11 worlds, 300+ levels, endless zones, mini-games, daily Piñata Party events and world-end boss encounters.

The useful design lens is not “new theme.” It is “new gameplay vocabulary and new combinations built on top of the player's existing knowledge.”

11 / REPLAYABILITY
REUSE THE RULES, CHANGE THE DECISIONS
Replayability is stronger when the same system can generate meaningfully different solutions.

LOADOUT

Different plant choices change available answers.

ENCOUNTER

Different compositions change what becomes urgent.

OBJECTIVE

Different goals shift which decisions are optimal.

12 / LIVE CONTENT
PERMANENT LEVELS + TEMPORARY ACTIVITIES

EA highlights daily Piñata Party events, Challenge Zones and Travel Log quests with scheduled and epic quest structures.

CORE LEVELS+DAILY / SCHEDULED+QUESTS+CHALLENGES

Live content can refresh player priorities while reusing the core ruleset.

ANALYTICS → DESIGN IMPLICATION

A larger content market raises the value of a repeatable content engine.

For a long-lived mobile strategy product, the design challenge is not simply adding more levels. It is creating a sustainable pipeline of new problems, new combinations, new progression value and temporary reasons to return while keeping the underlying rules readable.

Market data is context, not causation. The useful question for a designer is: what content architecture allows a stable core ruleset to support a large, long-lived player journey?

15 / WHAT I LEARNED
LEVEL DESIGN IS ALSO TEACHING DESIGN.
01

Difficulty can come from combination.

Known threats become harder when the designer changes context, timing or competition for attention.

02

New content should create new decisions.

A new plant or zombie has more design value when it expands the solution space.

03

Progression and level design should feed each other.

Player capabilities create new possibilities for future encounters.

04

Objectives are efficient content multipliers.

A different goal can change player behavior without rebuilding the rules.

05

Content pacing controls perceived difficulty.

The spacing between teaching, testing and escalation matters as much as raw challenge.

06

Live content can extend a stable core.

Temporary activities can refresh priorities while preserving the player's learned vocabulary.

16 / FINAL DESIGN STATEMENT

Good level design does not only ask “How difficult should this level be?” It asks “What does the player know now, what can they do now, and what new problem can I create from that knowledge?”
